# When does ML actually help LP dispatch? An honest characterization

*A predict-then-correct pipeline for production-cost dispatch, measured until it broke — where the error actually lives, three negative results with nailed mechanisms, and the case that ±1% is a solver result, not a prediction result.*

---

## Summary

Production-cost dispatch — multi-period DC optimal power flow with storage coupling — is solved exactly but slowly: the monolithic LP grows super-linearly in horizon × network size, so practice segments the horizon and accepts the error. Machine learning promises to shortcut the whole thing with one forward pass. The literature mostly reports where that promise works. This study characterizes where it doesn't, why it doesn't, and exactly what the ML *should* predict so that an exact solver can do the rest cheaply.

Seven results, each stated with its replication status:

1. **A whole-horizon, derivative-aware transformer is a ~5–6× better predictor than a pointwise (temporally blind) gradient-boosting baseline** on the predictor-only cost gap at month horizon — replicated across three independent data draws with error bars (HGBM +257±39% vs transformer +44±8%; the draws vary load and line ratings on the fixed 200-bus demo topology, not the network). Cost rides the *injection* (the SoC derivative), which a pointwise model structurally cannot shape — the claim is whole-horizon-vs-pointwise, not transformer-vs-every-ML-baseline. **[3-seed, ±err, single topology]**
2. **The deployed cost gap is seam-bound, not prediction-bound.** Per-cell injection error is flat across horizon (RMSE 0.034–0.038 from one month to one year) while the deployed gap grows 4.2%→22.7% — the growth rides the corrector's seam count (sub-linearly: the per-seam contribution falls ~0.56→0.25 %/seam over that span), not prediction quality, and a perfect-prediction control sits at the metric floor at every chunk size. **[single-seed across the horizon axis; the T=8760 endpoint is an N=40 pool one congested instance below our own §2 stability floor; the flat control at every chunk size and two horizons is the internal-consistency evidence]**
3. **The predictor is not the ±1% lever — three families of predictor-side fixes fail, each with a nailed mechanism.** A decision-focused, price-weighted loss is monotonically *worse* (there is no misallocation for it to redirect); a tokenized cross-entropy head and its autoregressive (GPT-style) decode both lose to plain MSE at toy scale under controlled multimodality (one under-commits, the other exposure-bias-collapses); and the root cause is measured, not conjectured: the per-unit prediction target is LP-degenerate — the same pipeline that caps at overfit R² 0.83 on the real target reaches 0.9999 on a synthetic well-determined one. **[γ-sweep single-seed; toy probes 2-seed; degeneracy diagnosis corroborated by two independent tracks, controls single-seed each]**
4. **±1% of the exact reference *is* reachable where we measured for it (T=720 and T=2160) — via the exact solver, at a modest speedup.** Re-coupling the horizon in ~2–4 exact chunks pinned to the predicted trajectory crosses ±1% at ~1.8–3.3× (T=720) and ~1.9–3.4× (T=2160) — each range spans the defensible monolith denominators (§4). Two scope notes: "±1%" means reaching the metric's own resolution floor (a perfect-prediction control reads −1.0%), not a certified distance from the true optimum; and at T=8760 ±1% stays reachable while the speedup erodes toward 1× (extrapolated, §4). Better prediction shrinks how much exact work is needed; it does not change reachability. **[n=7 congested pool, single draw, ±2pp seam-placement noise, self-flagged]**
5. **What the ML should predict is the *learnable* thing: the degeneracy-invariant aggregate.** The aggregate storage trajectory reaches holdout R² 0.96 where the per-unit target caps at 0.83, and realized as a banded warm-start constraint — solver owns the split — it lands +1.3% uncongested / +3.8% congested, with the band width as an explicit trust/speed knob. **[R²: 352-instance train, single split; banded-cost validation: 8 instances, single draw]**
6. **The honest comparison: at 5-hour storage, the industry's overlapping rolling horizon is near-optimal where we benchmarked it head-to-head (T=2160), and our decomposition does not beat it there.** The mechanism is worth more than the scoreline: the industry overlap is predictor-free and self-correcting, while an ML-pinned seam *trusts* its anchor — a cheap global re-solve beats a learned anchor unless the anchor is genuinely good at that horizon. The regime where a learned whole-horizon anchor can win (long-duration storage, where segmentation goes horizon-blind) is characterized analytically and pre-registered as ongoing work — with the experiment's decision rules, kill conditions, and two probe-killing engine bugs published *before* the run. **[overlap benchmarked at one horizon; instance-matched n=4, single-seed; the naive no-overlap variant reads 5.6–12.6% across T=2160–8760]**
7. **Metric honesty as methodology.** A ground-truth control through the identical cost path scores ≈0% on every pool where it was run (three of the four horizons) — the metric's own resolution is ~±1%, so every reported gap is prediction quality, not artifact. The pooled congested metric needs ~80 instances (~7 congested) before it stops lying by multiples. One published internal attribution was retracted when a control showed the artifact was in the reconstruction, not the claimed cause; the retraction and the control that caught it are part of the record. **[ground-truth controls at T=720/2160/8760; not run at T=4320]**

Everything runs on an open stack — scipy + HiGHS, a custom sparse DC-OPF engine, synthetic instance generation, consumer GPU for training, zero proprietary data, zero external data dependencies. The point of this paper is not that ML fails: it is that *knowing exactly where the boundary sits* — and stating each number with the evidence class behind it — is what makes the working configurations trustworthy.

## 1. The question and the testbed

The workload is production-cost dispatch: given a network, generator fleet, storage, and an hourly load trajectory over a horizon of T hours, minimize total generation cost subject to power balance, line limits, and storage state-of-charge (SoC) dynamics. As a monolithic LP this is exact — and it explodes. Storage couples every hour to the next, so the horizon cannot be cut without cost; the LP grows to hundreds of thousands of variables at month horizons on a 200-bus network (255,600 variables at T=720; ~767k at T=2160).

**The monolith wall, measured.** On our 200-bus congested testbed, monolithic solve times run one to three minutes at T=720 and six to twelve minutes at T=2160. Two honesty notes attach to that sentence. First, the corpus behind this paper accumulated *three different* congested-pool T=720 monolith timings — 64s, 112s, 152s — from three harnesses on the same class of instances (a fourth, 73.1s/scenario, appears in §6's screening study on a mixed congested+uncongested pool — a different population, kept apart deliberately). A dedicated re-pin run for this paper (one harness, sequential solves on an otherwise idle machine, every re-solve exactness-checked against its cached optimum) shows per-instance variance alone is large enough to account for that spread: five congested instances at T=720 span 61–151s inside one harness (median 85.8s). A harness contribution is *not excluded* — the re-pin never crossed the same instance between harnesses — which is exactly why every speedup ratio in this paper is computed within one harness on stated instances, never across two. Second, the super-linearity exponent: the re-pin gives α ≈ 1.6 on pooled medians (85.8s at T=720, n=5 → 494.8s at T=2160, n=3), with same-instance pairs spanning α ≈ 1.66–1.93 (wall ∝ T^α; three paired instances). Where a conclusion depends on α (§7), we quote the conservative range α ∈ [1.3, 2.1] that brackets every harness pair in the corpus — the in-harness value sits inside it. **[in-harness re-pin, n=5/n=3, single machine, sequential, exactness-gated]**

**What practice does.** Industry does not solve the monolith at long horizons; it segments — overlapping monthly rolling horizon, solving ~730-hour blocks with a 1–2-week look-ahead overlap that is re-solved and discarded. On this testbed, at the storage duration the demo runs (5-hour), that method is *near-optimal where we benchmarked it head-to-head*: +1.47% at 348s (lag-168 overlap) and ~0% at 536s (lag-336) at T=2160, versus that harness's ~651s monolith **[single-seed pool; the overlap variant was benchmarked at this one horizon]**. Even *naive* no-overlap monthly segmentation stays at 5.6–12.6% across T=2160–8760 on our pools, and the overlap's self-correction mechanism (§7) is the reason practice trusts it across horizons — an inference from mechanism, where the cross-horizon claim is concerned, not a measurement. Any claim that ML beats "industry practice" has to beat *that*, not a strawman of naive slicing. Most of this paper is about what we learned by failing to.

## 2. Testbed and metric honesty

**The engine.** A sparse multi-period DC-OPF LP (scipy + HiGHS): per-hour KCL and line limits replicated across the horizon via Kronecker products, plus SoC balance rows coupling consecutive hours for each of 25 storage units. Two networks carry the results: a 200-bus system with line ratings scaled to 55% (heavily congested — the hard case) and a 400-bus system at natural ratings (the representative case). Load trajectories come from a realistic generator (multi-week structure: heatwaves, seasonal drift) — this matters, because the simple diurnal profiles used in early experiments turn out to be a *different problem* (a 5% SoC noise floor costs +8% on diurnal profiles vs +298% on realistic ones at T=720; the compounding catastrophe lives entirely in the long-range structure).

**Scope, stated up front.** Every congested cost number in this paper comes from this one synthetic 200-bus topology and this one synthetic load generator; the "independent data draws" behind the replicated headline vary load and line ratings on that fixed topology. Cross-topology and real-load generalization are untested here — the multi-topology question belongs to planned follow-on work, and the load-structure sensitivity we measured (the 37× diurnal-vs-realistic gap above) is exactly why we won't extrapolate silently.

**The metric, defined once.** All cost gaps are **pooled congested cost gap**: (Σ method cost − Σ monolith cost)/Σ monolith cost over the congested instances of a holdout pool, pure generation cost on both sides, same HiGHS solver on both sides, heavy solves run serially. "feas" = predictor-only (feasibility projection, no cost repair). "corrected" = the deployed tile corrector at a stated chunk size. Speedup = monolith wall / method wall, same harness.

**The floor: the metric can't resolve below ~±1%.** Ground-truth monolith SoC pushed through the identical evaluation path scores ≈0% on every pool — T=720 feas/tile −1.00%; T=8760 feas +0.5% / tile −0.08% **[N=40 pool at T=8760]** — and the perfect-prediction control is *flat at every chunk size* (−1.00% at T=720, −0.75% at T=2160). Two consequences: every reported gap is prediction quality, not metric artifact or corrector ceiling; and "±1%" is right at the edge of what this yardstick can distinguish — treat sub-1% differences as ties.

**The N rule: pooled congested cost needs ~80 instances.** The pooled congested metric is dominated by a handful of expensive instances; at N=32 (2 congested in eval) it inflated 2–5×, up to +2600% garbage on the worst cells. N≈80–120 with ~7 congested eval instances is where it stabilizes — we watched the same pipeline produce +59.5% (N=32) and +21.3% (N=120) for the same method at T=2160. Any surrogate study reporting pooled cost gaps on a handful of instances is reporting noise; we suggest the field treat ~7 congested eval instances as a floor, not a target. **[measured at T=2160/4320/8760 via paired N=32 vs N=120/40 pools]**

**Measurement traps (the box we wish we'd had at the start).** Each of these produced a wrong number before it produced a rule:

1. Measure the hard axis — buses × **horizon**, not just buses. T=24 is the easy case that flatters everything.
2. Compare **pure generation cost** on both sides. The monolith objective carries tie-break terms; comparing it against a method's pure gen cost fabricates gaps (we saw spurious −72%).
3. Anchor the pipeline on the **SoC trajectory**, not injection reconstructed from it (reconstruction drifts over long horizons).
4. Run heavy solves **serially**. Parallel contention inflated a baseline 128s→1442s — an ~11× phantom.
5. Keep tie-break penalties at validated values: `soc_penalty=1e-5` triggered an LP-degeneracy slowdown (1876s vs 128s at 433k variables).
6. Correction windows must pin **both** SoC endpoints; entering-only windows "borrow" energy and report negative gaps.
7. A feasibility-only correction loop fixes feasibility, **not optimality** — a feasible-but-expensive schedule sails through uncorrected.

## 3. Result I — the predictor: whole-horizon transformer vs pointwise GBM

**Headline (the replicated one).** At T=720 on the congested pool, predictor-only (feas) cost gaps across three independent data draws:

| model | feas gap (3-seed mean±std) |
|---|---|
| HGBM (pointwise gradient boosting) | **+257±39%** |
| whole-horizon transformer, plain MSE (λ0) | **+90±9%** |
| + derivative-aware loss + level anchor (λ32β1) | **+44±8%** |

The transformer cuts the pointwise baseline's gap by ~65%; the derivative-aware loss roughly halves what remains — a ~5–6× total gap ratio over HGBM, stable across draws. The λ-sweep is monotone (feas +90→+44% as λ goes 0→32) and the mechanism metric moves with it (derivative RMSE 0.0457→0.0382). **[3-seed, ±err]**

**Mechanism: cost rides the injection.** The interventional evidence is the λ-sweep itself: pushing loss weight onto the derivative monotonically cuts both derivative RMSE and cost, replicated across three draws. Corroborating correlations (single-seed, n=20 eval) point the same way: per-instance absolute $ gap tracks injection (ΔSoC) RMSE at **0.88**; SoC-level RMSE at **0.65**. Plain MSE-on-level over-smooths — compression slope 0.793, i.e. the model pulls ~21% toward the mean and shaves both SoC rails — which barely dents the *level* metric while wrecking the *derivative* at exactly the charge/discharge peaks where money moves. The derivative-aware loss is therefore a structural regularizer, not a tradeoff: SoC R² *rose* when it was added. This is also why a pointwise GBM is structurally out of the game: it cannot shape a trajectory derivative it never sees. One honest boundary on the headline: no *sequence-aware non-transformer* baseline (LSTM, temporal CNN, GBM on windowed/derivative features) was run — the measured claim is whole-horizon-vs-pointwise, and the mechanism says the win comes from seeing the trajectory, not from the transformer per se. **[λ-monotonicity 3-seed; correlations single-seed corroboration]**

**The per-horizon envelope, verified against artifacts.** Trained at each horizon and evaluated on the same pools, the predictor-only advantage over HGBM holds across the measured range — with its size depending on horizon:

| T | HGBM feas | transformer feas | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 720 | +253% | +35% | 7.2× |
| 2160 | +592% | +81% | 7.3× |
| 4320 | +590% | +98% | 6.0× |
| 8760 | +589% | +166% | 3.5× |

**[single-seed per horizon; n=7 congested eval at 720/2160/4320; the T=8760 row is an N=40 pool with 6 congested eval — one congested instance below our own §2 stability floor, so read it as indicative; the T=720 point is the one replicated 3-seed, where the single-seed draw here (7.2×) sits on the favorable tail of the replicated ~5–6× (257/44 = 5.8×)]**. Two honest notes. At T=168 the corpus only has the *plain-MSE* transformer comparison (+43% vs +23%, ~1.9×) — the derivative-loss model was never run there, so we do not claim the envelope below T=720. And on the *deployed corrected* metric the ratio compresses to 0.9–2.5× — the corrector floors both models, which is not a discrepancy but the first sighting of §4's main result: deployed cost is not predictor-bound.

**The feature axis is closed (negative, twice).** Two hand-engineered feature families — timestep-level injection-peak drivers (ramps, causal rolling z-scores, cumulative imbalance integrals) and instance-level cluster cost-extremity — both *hurt* on every metric (feas +41%→+47% and +45% respectively; R² down 0.038 and 0.047). Diagnosis: attention over the raw load sequence already computes the timestep structure internally (hand-copies dilute capacity), and the instance-level structure is already inferable from inputs the model has. The winning lever was the objective (the derivative loss), not the inputs. Structure does the lift. **[single-seed, but the two independent negatives agree]**

## 4. Result II — the seam-bound gap

This is the paper's core finding, and the reason most intuitions about "better predictor → better deployed cost" fail on this workload.

**The deployment pipeline.** The predictor emits the whole-horizon SoC trajectory; fixing it decouples the horizon into independent per-hour exact solves; a corrector then re-couples where it matters: **tile mode** re-solves the contiguous congested span in chunks of at most `chunk_max` hours, each chunk pinned at both ends to the predicted SoC, with a monotone incumbent (a change is accepted only if the exact re-solved cost improves) and both-ends pinning guaranteeing gap ≥ 0. An arbitrage-potential gate — one network-free storage LP pass, cost independent of bus count — decides whether any of this fires at all: on the natural 400-bus network it idles (gate values 0.19–0.26 vs threshold 0.5) and the pipeline's month-horizon speedup there passes through untouched — 167×/168× in the gate-validation run, whose monolith timed at 879s; a prior run read 197× on a slower monolith timing (949s) — the same denominator sensitivity §1 flags. On the congested network the gate fires (2.5–13.7, separation growing with horizon). **[gate values measured per horizon, single instances]**

**The decomposition.** Across horizons, per-cell predictor error is flat while deployed cost grows:

| T | injection RMSE | corrected gap (chunk 96) | perfect-SoC control |
|---|---|---|---|
| 720 | 0.0380 | +4.2% | −1.00% (flat at every chunk size) |
| 2160 | 0.0356 | +11.7% | −0.75% (flat at every chunk size) |
| 4320 | 0.0366 | +13.3% | — |
| 8760 | 0.0335 | +22.7% | −0.08% (tile) |

The growth cannot be prediction quality (flat RMSE), and cannot be the corrector's mechanics (the perfect-prediction control is flat at every chunk size at both horizons where we swept it). What grows is the **seam count**: at chunk 96 the corrector plants ~T/96 seams (7 at T=720; ~91 at T=8760), each pinned to the *predicted* SoC. Two disciplined qualifications on our own favorite result. The growth is *sub-linear* in seam count — the per-seam contribution falls ~0.56→0.25 %/seam over 720→8760 (0.56→0.52→0.30 across the strictly comparable N=120 pools) — so "seam-bound" is the right qualitative reading (the controls exclude worse per-cell prediction and corrector mechanics), but no clean per-seam constant falls out of the data, and the predictor's *level* error (R² 0.74→0.32 over the same horizons), which the corrector pins at those same seams, is a co-cause we cannot separate here. And the largest growth point sits on the weakest pool. **[single-seed across the horizon axis; T=8760 = N=40 pool below the §2 stability floor — the least reliable growth point, not the strongest; the flat floor at every chunk size × two horizons is the strength of the evidence]**

**The ±1% frontier.** If the gap is seam-bound, fewer seams should buy accuracy at the price of speed — and they do: ±1% needs a roughly **constant chunk count (~2–4 chunks; the measured crossings land between ~T/4 and ~T/2)**, not a constant chunk size. The frontier is bumpy enough that the midpoint divisor T/3 itself reads +2.9% at T=2160 — the chunk *count* is the rule; no particular divisor is.

| horizon | ±1% crossing | wall | monolith reference | speedup at ±1% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T=720 | chunk 240: **+0.76%** | 46.4s | 86–152s (see note) | **~1.8–3.3×** |
| T=2160 | chunk 480–1080: **+1.07% → +0.24%** | 194–265s | 495–651s (see note) | **~1.9–3.4×** |

A denominator note the field usually buries — and that an earlier draft of this paper buried for the T=720 row: *neither* row has a single clean denominator. At T=720 the frontier harness's monolith reference (152s) is the mean of just two congested instances sitting at the very top of the re-pin's per-instance span (61–151s, median 85.8s), and the 46.4s numerator is a mean over the full 20-instance eval while the gap pools only its 7 congested — so we quote **~1.8× against the re-pin median up to ~3.3× against the harness's own two-instance pair**. At T=2160 the frontier harness's own retime ran anomalously slow (contention, flagged in source); the sibling-harness value is ~651s and the idle-machine re-pin median is 495s — so **~1.9–2.5× against the re-pin median, ~2.5–3.4× against the retime**. The qualitative claim — ±1% at a modest, small-multiple speedup — survives every defensible denominator; the second decimal does not, so we don't print one.

The frontier is bumpy at n=7 congested (+2.90% at chunk 720 sits between +1.07% at 480 and +0.24% at 1080 — which hour a seam lands on swings one dominant instance by percentage points; we flag ±2pp of seam-placement noise rather than smoothing it). The speedup is *modest and roughly horizon-stable* at 720→2160 — a few chunks of length T/3 beat one length-T monolith by LP super-linearity — and erodes toward T=8760, where each ~2900h chunk rivals a whole shorter-horizon monolith **[the 8760 speedup statement is extrapolated, not measured — we say so]**. At the fast operating point (chunk 96) the gap floors at ~4% / ~10% / ~23% at 720/2160/8760: fast-and-loose is available, fast-and-±1% is not. **[n=7 congested, single draw]**

**The corrector itself.** Tile mode is what closes cost — and its development-track numbers must be read for what they are: they use a *synthetic anchor* (ground-truth SoC + 5% noise, whose error does not grow with horizon), not the deployed transformer. Under that anchor, tile(96) takes +298% to **+5.0% at 4.1×** (T=720) and reads +0.6%/+1.2% at 7.3×/8.7× at 2–3 months — evidence that the corrector's *speedup* grows with horizon because the chunk tracks the storage arbitrage timescale (diurnal, at 5h storage), not the horizon. Under the *deployed transformer anchor*, whose error is horizon-degrading, the same tile(96) reads +4.2%/+11.7%/+22.7% at 720/2160/8760 — the decomposition table above. Reconciled: the corrector is horizon-robust *given a horizon-stable anchor*; the deployed pipeline's anchor isn't one at long horizon, and that difference is this section's whole point. Design ranking, measured: **tile > flag > price > sweep** — contiguous re-coupling beats fragmented flagged-hours re-coupling (+5.0% vs +264% at T=720: scattered cheap-looking hours fragment the windows into noisy seams; the chunk sizes differ — tile(96) vs flag(72) — but tile's own chunk-size sensitivity is ~1pp over 96→168, two orders below that gap, so contiguity is the driver), which beats LMP-arbitrage repair (storage can't capture congestion rent as a price-taker), which beats free-exit chaining (a greedy per-chunk dump loses the anchor's value). The deeper lesson: even a noisy predicted trajectory is a valuable *global anchor* — re-coupling pinned to it beats re-deriving the schedule from prices. **[single-instance frontier probes; the +298→+5.0 headline reproduces to the decimal across two harnesses]**

## 5. Result III — the predictor is NOT the ±1% lever

Three families of predictor-side fixes, three negatives, three mechanisms. We report these at full strength: a measured negative with a nailed mechanism is a result, not an apology.

### 5.1 Decision-focused (price-weighted) loss: monotonically worse

The obvious next move after §4 is to make the predictor care about cost where cost lives. LP duality hands us the gradient for free: by the envelope theorem, ∂cost/∂injection = −LMP, and the monolith's nodal prices are cached per training instance — so a price-weighted injection loss (weight |LMP|^γ) is the first-order linearization of true dispatch-cost loss, with zero extra solves and no differentiable-solver machinery. It fails cleanly:

| γ | pw-injRMSE (its own target) | corrected gap |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (baseline) | 0.0397 | **+4.19%** |
| 1 | 0.0396 | +5.26% |
| 2 | 0.0396 | +5.54% |

The weighting cannot improve *its own objective* (pw-injRMSE moves by 0.0001) because the baseline's injection error is already ~uniform across price levels — there is no cheap-hours-over-served/expensive-hours-under-served misallocation to redirect. It just trades bulk accuracy for a high-LMP gain that never materializes. Decision-focused reweighting needs a misallocation to exploit; here there isn't one. (RL against the same objective was deprioritized on the same grounds: same near-saturated target, higher variance, and no differentiable-solver blocker to route around — the dual already supplies the gradient.) Scope, honestly: this kills the *linearized, dual-weighted* family on this target. Heavier decision-focused machinery — SPO+-style surrogates, differentiable solver layers — was not run; the no-misallocation measurement is the reason we expect it to fail the same way here, but that expectation is an inference, and §9's boundary-case offer to the DFL literature is scoped to what we tested. **[γ-sweep, single-seed, T=720; harness anchor: γ=0 reproduces the known baseline to the decimal]**

### 5.2 The root cause, measured: the target is LP-degenerate

Why is the error uniform and irreducible? Because a chunk of the target isn't a function of the inputs at all. The dispatch LP distributes storage duty across 25 near-equivalent units; the split among them is **degenerate** — identical inputs, different optimal per-unit SoC, same cost. Two controls pin this: the pipeline **overfit-caps at R² 0.83** on the real per-unit target — it cannot even *memorize* it — while the same pipeline reaches **0.9999** on a synthetic target that *is* a well-determined function of the same inputs. The load-bearing contrast is within-pipeline: the same machinery reaches **0.96 on the aggregate** (§6) while capping at 0.83 per-unit — the *sum* is learnable, only the *split* is not, which is the fingerprint of degeneracy rather than capacity. The model's over-smoothing is not a pathology; it is the correct hedge on an unlearnable component. Two independent tracks converge on this (the control ladder above; the §5.1 loss-lever failure, which is what degeneracy looks like from the cost side). This diagnosis does real work: it predicts which targets are learnable (§6) and which fixes are doomed (§5.3–5.4). **[replicated across two tracks; controls single-seed each]**

### 5.3 Architecture: helps in-distribution, fails where it was needed

Swapping the learned position encoding for sinusoidal cuts the T=2160 corrected gap 12.0%→10.25% (−15%; the 12.0% is that comparison track's own learned-PE baseline, which reproduces §4's main-sweep 11.7% within run-to-run variation) with *unchanged* R²/injection-RMSE — it relocates the same-size errors to less costly places. But the hoped-for prize — train at 2160, deploy at 8760 — fails on cost: the derivative transfers (injection RMSE 0.0374 vs the 8760-trained 0.0335) while the absolute level collapses (R² 0.033), and since the corrector pins *level* at its seams, the length-generalized model is worse than training directly at the horizon (+28.9% vs +22.7%). A real but modest in-distribution win; no free lunch across horizons. **[single-seed]**

### 5.4 What doesn't fix it, part two: tokenization and autoregression (the GPT hypothesis)

If MSE's conditional mean is the problem (it smears multimodal decisions), a categorical head over quantile-binned injections should *commit* to a mode; if one-pass prediction lacks coherence, a GPT-style autoregressive decode should restore it. Both hypotheses were tested at toy scale under *manufactured, controlled* multimodality — a degenerate two-unit split cell and a hidden-latent spike-timing cell, exactly the failure modes measured on the big corpus — cheaply, before any big-corpus spend. Both lose to plain MSE, for different, control-isolated reasons:

- **One-pass cross-entropy head:** argmax decoding collapses to the near-zero modal bin — it *under*-commits (discharging substantially in only ~10% of instances), breaking the aggregate on the split cell (+37.7% vs MSE +2.2%) and failing the timing cell (+58.4% vs +25.0%). Expected-value decoding is definitionally the conditional mean — it re-averages back into MSE. A sampling decode recovers amplitude but breaks cross-unit coordination and still loses. K-robust from 16 to 256 bins; two seeds. **[toy scale, 2 data seeds, 3-lens adversarial verification]**
- **Autoregressive CE decode:** *fixes* the amplitude collapse (swing amplitude 0.38→0.98, commit rate 0.10→1.00) — and still loses (+35.4% vs MSE +25.0%), because of textbook exposure bias, measured directly: teacher-forced +6.2% vs free-run +35.4%, a **+29-point gate** that scheduled sampling barely dents (+33.4%). The sharpest control: an oracle selector over 16 sampled trajectories reaches +3.1% — the model's distribution *contains* near-optimal trajectories — but the best deployable selector (max log-prob) only reaches +30.3%. The failure is *selection under self-conditioning*, not representational capacity. And AR-over-time is orthogonal to the cross-unit split (it collapses to the all-zero state there): that axis needs a joint-over-units decode or the aggregate route of §6, not sequence order. **[toy scale, 2 data seeds, 2-lens adversarial verification, upheld]**

The idea wasn't empty: commitment is *economically real* — a perfect committer beats a perfect smearer (+19.15% vs +23.0%, both seeds) — but it is a joint/sequential property that no realizable decode we tested can reach, and the hidden-latent cell caps *any* committer at +19% regardless. Both probes reinforce the standing result instead of softening it: the ±1% work belongs to the exact solver.

### 5.5 The retraction, told straight

One attribution in this program was published internally and then retracted, and the mechanism of the catch is worth more than the tidied record. An analysis attributed a +7.7% cost gap on the aggregate target to "the spatial storage split under congestion." A control — distributing the *ground-truth* aggregate by even split, no learning anywhere — read +10.3% on *uncongested* instances vs +7.6% congested: higher where the explanation said it should be near zero. The artifact was the even-split *reconstruction* (uniform cycling + piecewise efficiency corrupts the implied injection), not congestion. The corrected claim (§6) was rebuilt from the control outward: constrain the solver to the predicted aggregate and let it choose the split. Control-before-attribution is, in our experience, the single highest-yield discipline in surrogate evaluation — every number in this paper that survived did so by passing a control of this shape, and one number here is only in the record because a control killed it. **[the control: single-seed, N=50 congested holdout; it kills the even-split reconstruction, not §6's banded realization]**

## 6. Result IV — what TO predict: the aggregate, realized by the exact solver

The degeneracy diagnosis (§5.2) says per-unit targets are unlearnable — and simultaneously says what is learnable: any degeneracy-*invariant* function of the solution. The obvious one is the **aggregate** storage trajectory (sum over units).

**The aggregate is learnable.** Holdout aggregate-R² **0.96** (352 training instances; 0.952 on congested instances) — versus the 0.83 *overfit ceiling* of the per-unit target. Horizon-compounding on the aggregate (0.84 at 30d → 0.35 at 180d) is fixed by multi-scale global temporal features — position-in-horizon, causal running means, weekly/monthly phase — recovering to 0.75 at 180d. **[single split; feature fix replicated across two horizon cells]**

**Realized as a banded warm-start constraint.** Don't reconstruct per-unit schedules from the aggregate (that's the retracted artifact of §5.5); *constrain* Σ_s soc[t,s] to lie within a band δ of the prediction and let the exact solver own the split. With the true aggregate, this recovers the monolith exactly (+0.00% on all 8 test instances, congested and not). With the *predicted* aggregate at δ=0.12: **+1.3% uncongested / +3.8% congested**, degrading gracefully as the band tightens (δ=0.03/0.06/0.12 → 10.6/4.2/1.3% uncongested). δ is an explicit trust/speed dial: tight = trust the ML and search less; wide = pay more solve for less trust. **[8-instance validation, single draw]**

**The exact micro-lever: basis warm-starts.** Inside the deployed pipeline's per-hour decouple, reusing the simplex basis across consecutive hours (slowly-varying RHS) solves each hour in ~0.24ms vs ~3.5ms cold — **~15×, at 2 simplex iterations per re-solve** — and *exactly*, but only after a trap worth publishing: at HiGHS's default feasibility tolerance (1e-7) a warm re-solve silently terminated 1.3e-5 short of optimal. Tightening primal/dual tolerances to 1e-10 restored agreement to <2e-6 at the same 2 iterations. Warm-start speed claims that don't state their tolerance settings should be read with suspicion — including ours. **[single-instance probe, exactness-gated vs cold scipy]**

**What a fast approximate trajectory buys in deployment: screening.** As a workload test of the "ML seeds, solver decides" division, we used the aggregate model as a Monte-Carlo *ranking* screen over 470 cached scenarios (118 holdout): push every scenario through a cheap cost conversion, keep the top-K, run the exact LP only there. The result splits exactly along this paper's thesis line: with a congestion-*blind* conversion (merit-order fill, 11.9ms/scenario, ~6,150× per scenario) the ML ranks well (ρ≈0.91) — but *ties a no-ML proxy* (merit dispatch of the raw load): nothing the storage schedule knows can be expressed through a copper-plate conversion. With a congestion-*aware* conversion (per-hour DC-OPF, 2.83s, ~26×) the same predicted schedule ranks at **ρ=0.986** with recall-of-worst-20% = 0.96, and its marginal contribution over a no-storage control is significant (paired Δρ +0.072, bootstrap CI [+0.04, +0.11]). (The speedup denominators here — 73.1s/scenario, n=8 — are per-scenario means over this study's *mixed* congested+uncongested holdout, a different population from §1's congested-only walls; the ratios are order-of-magnitude by construction.) The ML earns its keep only once the evaluation can see the network — it is a seed for a cheap exact-ish solve, not a cost authority. **[single topology, N=118 holdout (50 congested), single draw, bootstrap CIs; the no-ML control is no-storage, not a best-cheap-heuristic]**

**The thesis, in one sentence: ±1% is a solver result, not a prediction result — ML's job is to make the exact solver's remaining work small and well-placed** (seed the aggregate; anchor the tiles; warm the bases).

## 7. The honest comparison — where industry methods stand

**At 5-hour storage, the overlapping rolling horizon wins where the within-harness comparison exists, and we say so.** Instance-matched at T=2160: industry lag-168 **+1.5% @ 348s** vs our decomposition at chunk 720 **+5.7% @ 327s** — strictly better accuracy at the same wall. The per-instance detail is more instructive than the pool: on one badly-predicted instance our tile pipeline stays +34% even at chunk 720 while the industry stitch nails it to +0.00%. One wrinkle we will not launder either way: a *cross-harness* read of our own frontier's chunk-1080 point (+0.24% @ 265s) against industry lag-336 (~0% @ 536s) would favor ours on wall — but those walls come from different harnesses and machine states, exactly the comparison §1 forbids, so we do not print it as a result. The verdict stands on the within-harness evidence it has (n=4, one operating point each), and settling the full frontier head-to-head — same pool, same harness, all operating points — is folded into the §8 probe. **[instance-matched n=4; single-seed]**

**The mechanism deserves to be printed, because it generalizes.** The industry overlap is *predictor-free and self-correcting*: each block re-solves a 1–2 week overlap that is then discarded, so a bad seam is re-optimized by physics and thrown away. Our tile corrector *pins* chunk endpoints to the ML prediction — each window is optimal-given-its-seams, so anchor error *survives* correction. A cheap global overlap re-solve beats trusting a learned anchor unless the anchor is genuinely good at that horizon. Seam-pinning converts predictor weakness into deployed cost exactly when the predictor is weakest — the long-horizon regime. This is our own method's sharpest measured liability, and any ML-anchored decomposition should expect it.

**Where a learned anchor can win — characterized, not claimed.** The industry method has one structural blind spot: each window is horizon-blind beyond its overlap. Let τ be the storage *arbitrage span* — the time distance between economically-linked charge and discharge decisions (set by duration *and* load structure, and value-weighted: if most arbitrage value sits at short spans, a partial overlap captures it). Then: for τ ≲ O (overlap), every kept decision sees its pair — near-optimal, which is precisely the measured 5h regime (τ ≈ diurnal ≪ 168–336h overlaps). For τ > O, decisions within τ−O of each block end are blind to their pair, and the error grows with the blind fraction and its economic weight. Restoring accuracy by lengthening the overlap costs (W+O)^α per window: with O ≈ τ = c·T, the wall ratio to the monolith is ≈ (T/W)·c^α — so there are **two distinct onsets, and they support different claims**: the *accuracy* onset at τ ≈ O (weeks, for affordable overlaps), and the *wall-inversion* onset only in the deep-seasonal band (τ ≳ 0.3T), where adequate overlap makes the segmented method as slow as the monolith it was avoiding. In the marginal band (50–200h storage), industry-with-adequate-overlap stays 1.7–60× *faster* than the monolith across the α range — the contest there is accuracy at deployable speed, not speed itself. **[analytic boundary from measured α range; pre-registered for measurement, not asserted]**

**And industry has a second answer we refuse to strawman.** Long-duration scheduling practice does not rely on overlap alone: coarse-to-fine hierarchical coupling is standard operational practice in hydro and seasonal-storage scheduling, and has been for decades — the water-value method traces to Stage & Larsson (1961), SDDP's expected-cost-to-go functions to Pereira & Pinto (1991), the Norwegian long-/medium-/short-term scheduling chain was documented as deployed utility practice by 1999 (Fosso et al.), and a 2023 comparative review confirms established model toolchains with inter-model information flow across Brazil, Norway, and the US (Helseth et al.; all ground-checked, §9). One precision matters: the standard practice hands down a *valuation* of end-of-horizon storage — water values, stochastic cost-to-go functions — not a pinned trajectory. The variant we benchmark (coarse daily-step monolith → boundary-SoC-pinned fine windows) is the *deterministic degenerate case* of that coupling: same coarse-to-fine shape, simpler object handed down. The coarse anchor is cheap and plausibly near-optimal on the smooth seasonal component; its honest open question is *congestion*: a daily-step model cannot see the extreme hours where — on a heavily congested network — the value of stored energy concentrates, so it may set the seasonal energy budget peak-blind. Whether that bias is ~1% or ~15% is genuinely empirical; neither our corpus nor the literature we found settles it. The symmetry is the sharpest way to put the whole contest: at long duration, *our transformer plays the same role as industry's coarse anchor* — a cheap whole-horizon approximation whose trajectory pins exact fine solves. The fair fight is anchor vs anchor: physics-feasible-but-peak-blind (coarse LP) versus full-resolution-but-approximate-and-horizon-specific (learned). They are quality-asymmetric in different directions, and which asymmetry dominates in the congested regime is exactly what the pre-registered probe (§8) measures.

## 8. Ongoing work — the pre-registered long-duration probe

Rather than promise future wins, we pre-register the experiment that decides the contest — decision rules, kill conditions, and the branch we'd rather not land on, all fixed before the run.

**Design.** One seasonal-gradient horizon (T=2160, seasonal load structure), storage duration swept D ∈ {5h, 500h} at the endpoints first, with cyclic storage; four engines per cell: (a) exact monolith; (b1) industry overlap-only at two overlaps; (b2) industry coarse-anchored (daily-step cyclic monolith → boundary-SoC-pinned monthly windows — built by swapping our tile corrector's anchor from ML to coarse-LP, which is itself a statement about how similar the two methods are); (c) ours, horizon-trained. Pooled congested metric under the §2 N rule; τ measured from the monolith trajectory rather than assumed; a value-density curve (cumulative arbitrage value vs pair distance, from the monolith duals) priced in *before* interpretation, because concentrated value lets a partial overlap stay accurate regardless of τ.

**Pre-registered decision rules.** DEGRADES = gap > 10% at wall ≤ 0.5× monolith; HOLDS = gap ≤ 3% at wall ≤ 0.5× monolith; 3–10% is a gray zone that goes to human adjudication with both readings written down, not self-adjudicated. Branches: b1 breaks but b2 holds → the claim *narrows* to anchor-vs-anchor and this paper's caveat section grows a measured curve; both degrade in the congested regime while ours holds → the differentiator claim earns full strength; ours also fails → the pivot dies and long-duration is characterized-not-conquered (with a data-adequacy control required before blaming the architecture); nobody degrades → the regime construction failed and gets at most two economics iterations before we stop. The demotion sentence for the most likely branch is pre-committed so the post-probe write-up can't rationalize.

**Two probe-killing bugs, caught on paper before any compute** — published because pre-mortems of this kind are cheaper than the runs they save. (1) The engine's `cyclic` storage flag was documented but *not implemented* — the docstring advertises it, the constraint builder never reads it; passing `cyclic=True` silently no-ops and reproduces a known free-SoC artifact where scaling up storage capacity gifts the optimizer free initial energy and collapses cost to $0. The fix (a circulant row-0 SoC balance, level left free) is specced with regression tests. (2) The SoC tie-break penalty becomes a *fake holding cost* at long duration: it scales with capacity × horizon jointly while generation cost doesn't, so it taxes exactly the seasonal arbitrage under study — in every engine *including the exact reference*, i.e. it corrupts the ground truth invisibly. And it is two-sided: setting it too low previously blew a 128s solve up to 1876s through LP degeneracy. The pre-registered gate compares SoC *trajectories* across penalty values, not objective shares — a <0.1% objective share can still distort the trajectory it taxes.

**Amortization economics, with the break-even stated.** The structural advantage that survives every probe branch is amortization: the predictor trains once per (topology, horizon, storage-config) class, while every LP-only method pays its full cascade per instance. Honestly priced, the per-class fixed cost is **not** just training (17min–4.1h on a consumer GPU) but label generation: every training label is a monolith solve, ~150–350 of them. And the deployed path prices new instances at the corrector-bound ±1% operating point (~46s at T=720; ~1.8–3.3× depending on the monolith denominator, §4) — not at "milliseconds" (pure inference misses ±1% by 4–23%). Break-even is therefore on the order of **~500–800 same-class ensemble members** at T=720 (computable either way from §4's denominators — a slower reference wall raises both the label cost and the per-instance saving; order-hundreds is the robust statement). The lane where this pays is large fixed-system Monte-Carlo and planning inner loops that re-solve one system class hundreds of times — not a 20–30-member contingency sweep. Cross-horizon transfer is measured to fail (§5.3), so the class boundary is real. **[fixed costs measured; break-even is arithmetic on measured quantities]**

## 9. Related work and positioning

Every citation below was fetch-verified before printing — most in a logged verification pass run for this paper (source page fetched, metadata confirmed, citing sentence checked against the work's own abstract, body-level claims flagged to their section), the remainder in an earlier logged citation vet whose corrections are honored here (one full text, the 2026 synergy-surrogate study, returned 403 in both passes and is characterized from its abstract). Where a check corrected our framing, the corrected framing is what appears.

**Amortized optimization and learning-to-optimize (the vocabulary home).** Our pipeline is an instance of amortized optimization — learning to accelerate the repeated solution of one problem family — for which Amos (2022, arXiv:2202.00665) is the tutorial and vocabulary reference. Kotary, Fioretto, Van Hentenryck & Wilder (IJCAI 2021 Survey Track, arXiv:2103.16378) survey the integration of ML with constrained-optimization solvers; our predictor-feeds-LP pipeline sits squarely on that map, and our contribution is not a new method on it but a characterization of where a natural point on it works and fails.

**Decision-focused learning (positioning the §5.1 negative).** Task-based end-to-end learning — train the predictor on the downstream decision objective rather than accuracy — anchors to Donti, Amos & Kolter (NeurIPS 2017, arXiv:1703.04529), who demonstrated it on grid scheduling and storage arbitrage; Elmachtoub & Grigas's SPO framework (*Management Science* 68(1), 2022, arXiv:1710.08005) builds the canonical decision-error loss from the same duality machinery our LMP-weighted loss uses. The modern survey and benchmark is Mandi et al. (JAIR 80:1623–1701, 2024, arXiv:2307.13565) — and its benchmark composition is the interesting fact for us: all seven benchmark problems predict *objective coefficients* with constraints fully known, and while decision-focused training usually matches or beats accuracy-based training, the advantage is not universal — several decision-focused methods underperform the prediction-focused baseline on the survey's portfolio-optimization problem. (The difficulty that literature wrestles with is loss-side, and it is elementary to state in our own words: an LP's argmin is piecewise-constant in its predicted coefficients, so exact regret gradients are zero almost everywhere — which is what the field's smoothed surrogate losses exist to fix.) Our negative is a different animal on both axes: the predicted quantity is a *decision variable* (storage SoC), and the failure is target-side — LP degeneracy makes the target partially unlearnable, so no loss reweighting can help (§5.2). To our knowledge this failure mode is not represented in the DFL benchmark suite; we offer it as a boundary case for the program: decision-focused learning presupposes a learnable target whose losses are misaligned, and degenerate LP solution components violate the presupposition. (That reading is ours, not the cited authors' — and it is scoped to the dual-weighted linearization we actually tested; SPO+-class surrogates and differentiable solver layers were not run on this target, §5.1.)

**Rolling-horizon error analysis (positioning §4 and §7).** The phenomenon our τ-vs-O boundary formalizes is documented empirically. End-of-horizon distortion in truncated multistage programs is classical (Grinold, *Operations Research* 31(3):407–431, 1983 — with energy planning already the motivating application). Sioshansi, Denholm, Jenkin & Weiss (*Energy Economics* 31(2):269–277, 2009) operate storage in two-week windows with a 15-day planning horizon precisely so end-of-window energy retains "carryover value" — a one-day overlap suppressing end-of-window drain — and show a *no-foresight backcasting rule* (dispatch on the previous two weeks' prices) captures ~85%+ of perfect-foresight arbitrage value. For long-duration storage, Guerra et al. (arXiv:2401.16605; *Renew. Sustain. Energy Rev.* 207:114940) benchmark dispatch approaches in production-cost models at 70–90% VRE shares and estimate better LDES dispatch modeling is worth 4–14% of operational value, stating explicitly (their §2) that the look-ahead window must exceed the storage's operational timeframe and that compute grows steeply with look-ahead length. Guigues & Sagastizábal (*EJOR* 217(1):129–140, 2012) establish rolling-horizon policies as cheap, practical (if formally sub-optimal) practice in hydro-thermal scheduling. What we did not find anywhere is an *analytic error decomposition* of the seam-bound form (seam count × fixed per-cell error) or an explicit boundary tying the storage arbitrage span to the overlap length — the field sizes overlaps empirically. Those two objects are this paper's contribution to that literature.

**Hierarchical coupling as practice (the §7 claim, ground-checked).** The water-value method traces to Stage & Larsson ("Incremental Cost of Water Power," *Trans. AIEE Part III* 80:361–364, 1961 — the reference the hydro-scheduling literature consistently cites as the origin); Pereira & Pinto (*Mathematical Programming* 52:359–375, 1991) introduced the method now known as SDDP, approximating expected-cost-to-go functions by piecewise-linear cuts, demonstrated on a 39-reservoir system; Fosso et al. (*IEEE Trans. Power Systems* 14(1):75–81, 1999) describe long-, medium- and short-term scheduling procedures with water-value computations as deployed Norwegian utility practice; Helseth et al. (*ASCE J. Water Resour. Plann. Manage.* 149(7), 2023) review established multi-model toolchains with inter-model information flow across Brazil, Norway, and the US Colorado system. Together these confirm §7's practice claim — with the precision noted there: practice hands down storage *valuations*, and our benchmarked coarse-anchor variant is the deterministic degenerate case of that coupling.

**Surrogates for energy planning models (the adjacent breadth).** Prina et al. (*Energy* 307:132735, 2024) train an ANN surrogate of EnergyPLAN to >99% accuracy (R²), the learning curve plateauing after only ~10 optimizer generations (their results, not the abstract). Kwon & Subramanyam's AutoSCEP (arXiv:2603.13508, 2026) surrogates scalar cost for capacity-expansion planning under uncertainty on EMPIRE at 2–8% gaps; it does not report an error-structure diagnosis. A 2026 *Smart Energy* study explains decision-variable synergies in an EnergyPLAN surrogate via XAI — the nearest neighbor to our diagnostic ambition, on a different object (which inputs interact, on cost, for EnergyPLAN) versus ours (where the deployed error lives — seams vs prediction — on dispatch). A 2025 review of AI in power-system planning (Yue et al., *Curr. Sustain./Renew. Energy Rep.*) names trustworthy-AI frameworks as an open challenge — a challenge papers like this one, built on controls and stated replication status, are trying to meet from below. On the dispatch side proper: MPA-DNN (arXiv:2510.09349, 2025) applies projection-aware unsupervised learning to exactly our problem class — multi-period DC-OPF with storage — as a positive-result method paper; LUMINA-Bench (arXiv:2605.02133, 2026) benchmarks AC-OPF surrogates and diagnoses where error concentrates (topologically: node-level error correlates with node degree, their §5.7) — single-period AC-OPF, diagnosing the *spatial* axis where we diagnose the *temporal* one; and Pagnier et al. (IREP 2022, arXiv:2205.11641) build a replace-the-solver digital twin of single-period quadratic-cost market clearing. None of these asks the question this paper asks.

**ML seeding exact solvers, and warm-start caution (positioning §6).** The lineage for "ML accelerates, the exact solver certifies" is Xavier, Qiu & Ahmed (*INFORMS J. Computing* 33(2):739–756, 2021, arXiv:1902.01697): learn redundant constraints, feasible starts, and likely-optimal subspaces from solved SCUC instances, hand them to the MIP solver — 4.3× average speedup with optimality guarantees preserved. Our §6 conclusion is that verdict, reached from the failure side, on LP dispatch, with the error decomposition explaining *why* the division of labor must fall that way. On warm starts specifically: interior-point warm-starting across related LPs is classical (Gondzio, *Mathematical Programming* 83:125–143, 1998); learned warm-start points are established for ACOPF (Baker, IEEE MLSP 2019, arXiv:1905.08860) and for fixed-point solvers with generalization bounds (Sambharya, Hall, Amos & Stellato, JMLR 25(166), 2024, arXiv:2309.07835). Most relevant to our discipline claim: Taheri & Molzahn (arXiv:2606.08984, 2026) benchmark warm starts for interior-point ACOPF and find they are *not* uniformly beneficial — most partial initializations slow the solver or hurt reliability. That is interior-point ACOPF, not simplex; but it is independent evidence for the posture our tolerance trap taught us: warm-start claims need explicit exactness gating. We did not find a published measurement of simplex *basis* reuse on sequences of per-hour DC-OPF LPs with slowly-varying RHS, nor documentation of the default-tolerance silent-early-termination trap; both appear to be new observations, offered with their single-instance status attached.

**Closest prior work, stated plainly.** No work we found combines (i) multi-period LP production-cost dispatch with storage, (ii) a seams-vs-prediction error decomposition, (iii) negative results with isolated mechanisms, and (iv) "when does ML help" as the explicit question. The nearest neighbors are Xavier et al. (the seed-the-solver verdict, on SCUC MIP, without error diagnosis), MPA-DNN (the problem class, as a method paper), and LUMINA (the diagnostic ambition, on single-period AC-OPF's spatial axis). We are not aware of a prior characterization of this shape; we state that as an absence-of-evidence claim after a deliberate search, not as a certainty.

## 10. Reproducibility and artifacts

Everything in this paper runs on an open stack: Python + scipy + HiGHS for every LP (same solver both sides of every comparison), PyTorch on a single consumer GPU for training, synthetic instance generation with cached pools (seeds recorded per pool), zero proprietary data, zero external data dependencies. The engine is a custom sparse multi-period DC-OPF implementation; per-result harnesses are single-file scripts (instance generation and caching; the transformer trainer and the derivative-aware loss; the tile corrector and its gate; the chunk frontier; the decision-focused sweep; the toy multimodality cells; the aggregate/banded warm-start validation; the ranking screen; the basis warm-start probe). Every headline figure traces to a named artifact file (JSON/checkpoint), and the artifact-level provenance is part of the repository release; per-figure replication status is tagged inline throughout.

Release form (full repo vs results-with-repro-instructions) is a publish-time decision; trained checkpoints can stay private without harming verifiability — the LP engine and harnesses are the load-bearing part. Two disclosed limits on that verifiability, so no reader discovers them for us. First, the checks in this paper are *internal*: claims are re-checkable by re-solving LPs, but with the same custom engine that produced them — a cross-solver validation against an independent implementation on a standard case is the pre-release gate we owe the repository, and §8's two paper-caught engine bugs are the argument for insisting on it. Second, the SoC tie-break penalty sits inside the reference argmin (though not in the reported pure-generation-cost metric); §8's pre-registered trajectory-level gate exists because its share grows with storage duration, and its trajectory effect at the horizons reported here has not been separately quantified.
